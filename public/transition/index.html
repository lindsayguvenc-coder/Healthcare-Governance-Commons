Core Concepts

These are Commons positions, not research findings. Use what fits your setting.

01
Foundation
The Good Container

Why the structure around a group matters as much as the people in it, and what a container that holds looks like.

The container is not an institution, not a building, not a committee. It's a shared space of trust, structure, and permission where serious work can happen without being crushed by politics, markets, fear, or ego.

Core properties

  • Legitimacy without capture — credible but not owned by governments, corporations, or ideologies
  • Psychological safety for radical honesty — participants can say uncomfortable truths without punishment
  • Time insulated from urgency — protected from quarterly thinking and short-term pressure
  • Cross-domain intimacy — not panels, but sustained collaboration across disciplines over months
  • Narrative incubation — generates the story of change, not just the mechanics
  • Stop authority — a named person can pause a tool now, without waiting for a meeting
  • Can say no — written power to reject, change and retire a tool, a budget, and a board committee it answers to. A group that can review but not refuse is paperwork.
  • Sees what's really in use — an inventory of the AI actually in use, including tools staff brought in themselves, gathered without punishing people for saying what they use

Failure modes

  • Ego capture — charismatic personalities absorb decision authority
  • Power drift — external interests gradually reshape the container's purpose
  • Groupthink — the container starts producing consensus instead of insight
  • Mission drift — original charter erodes as the group scales
  • Weaponization — the container's tools are turned against its original purpose
  • Rubber stamp — the group reviews everything and stops nothing, often because too few, too stretched people do the reviewing

The goal

A container doesn't need to be perfect. It needs to catch problems early and correct them quickly.

02
AI Partnership
AI as Immune System

Not the brain. Not the authority. The immune system — detecting drift, flagging imbalance, protecting the health of the container from the inside.

Treating AI as an arbiter or authority invites backlash, weaponization, and power struggles. The design used here is AI as process steward, humans as stewards of purpose. This is a design position, not a description of what today's tools reliably do.

The role we'd give AI

  • Epistemic hygiene — help spot contradictions and untested assumptions
  • Participation equity monitoring — help track who speaks, who decides, who benefits
  • Pattern detection — help notice slow drift people get used to
  • Disagreement classification — help sort a conflict into its parts
  • Institutional memory — help preserve decision history, audit trail, original intent
  • Early warning — help flag power consolidation and language drift early

What AI should not do

  • Decide outcomes
  • Hold moral authority
  • Override human judgment on values
  • Police or punish

The immune system metaphor

An immune system doesn't run the body. It detects threats early, signals the system, and enables correction, and it can also attack the body it protects. AI in a governance container needs the same limits.

03
Operating Principle
Disagreement as Data

When two thoughtful people disagree, the disagreement can reveal something that isn't visible from one perspective alone.

Disagreement often gets treated as a threat: something to resolve quickly, avoid, or win. When that happens, the information it contains can get lost.

What a disagreement can contain

  • Facts — what each side believes is true that could be checked
  • Assumptions — unstated beliefs about how things work
  • Values — what each side is trying to protect
  • Lived experience — what each side has seen firsthand
  • Incentives — what each side is rewarded or penalized for
  • Risks — what each side fears could go wrong
  • Time horizons — how far ahead each side is looking
  • Definitions — the same word meaning different things to each side
  • Who carries the cost — who holds the blame, money or harm if the decision goes wrong, and whether that is the person deciding. When the cost lands on one person and the group shares none of it, the safe move for that person is not to decide.

Use the Disagreement Unpacker tool above to walk through these questions with AI support on any real disagreement.

04
Implementation
Micro-decisions & Behavioral Architecture

Policies alone rarely change culture. Daily incentives usually matter more. This is the implementation layer of a charter.

A charter needs an implementation layer, where values become daily micro-decisions reinforced by the environment.

How it works

Values → rules of engagement → behavioral reinforcement loops → internalized norms → self-sustaining culture.

Positive reinforcement over policing

  • Catch people doing it right, not wrong
  • Make the desired behavior easier than the alternative
  • Name the behavior when it happens — visibility creates replication
  • Protect dissent publicly so people learn it's safe

What breaks it

  • A single retaliation event, which can undo months of work on psychological safety
  • Leaders not modeling the behavior they're reinforcing
  • Rewarding outcomes over process when the process is what matters
  • Moving too fast to notice the micro-signals

Culture forms through these repeated small decisions.

Charter Principles

Six principles for making decisions under uncertainty. Each one has a rule, and a failsafe for when the rule fails.

Dignity before efficiency
No policy may optimize total output by concentrating harm in a subgroup.
Failsafe: Automatic pause and redesign trigger if harm concentrates above threshold.
Psychological safety
Disagreement and uncertainty are protected behaviors. Retaliation is disqualifying.
Failsafe: Independent ombud + immediate review of any retaliation claim.
Disagreement as data
Every disagreement gets unpacked: facts, assumptions, values, lived experience, incentives, risks, time horizons, definitions, who carries the cost.
Failsafe: If recurring 3+ cycles → forced escalation and redesign.
Radical honesty
All decisions must declare uncertainty. Assumptions require owner, confidence range, and expiry date.
Failsafe: Decisions auto-expire if assumptions are not refreshed.
Equity and representation
No decision without presence of materially affected populations. A decision needs enough people present in two ways: enough members overall, and enough people from each group the decision affects. Patients are largely left out of US AI governance today, and including them is feasible.
Failsafe: Vote invalidated if representation gaps exist.
Dual-horizon discipline
Every proposal needs a plan for now (what keeps people safe in the short term) and a plan for later (what fixes the underlying problem).
Failsafe: Rejection if one horizon is achieved by sacrificing the other.
